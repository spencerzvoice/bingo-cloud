SZV: the Allan Peters method

I ran your SZV through Allan Peters' published process (designer behind Peters Design Company, author of Logos That Last) and made the calls the way he describes making them. Where his own words drive a decision, the step says so.

1 · Brand noun listBefore drawing anything, list what the mark could be, so you don't design stars for a client who hates stars.
2 · Rough sketches, wideUgly on purpose, lots of them. He shows clients 15 vector sketches, not 3, so the one they "wished for" is already on the table.
3 · Combine ideas"Usually it's multiple ideas": two nouns in one mark, using negative space, overlapping geometry, visual rhythm, visual flow.
4 · Narrow, reduce, testPick a top 3 by gut, strip each until it stops communicating, then check the curves, stroke weight and spacing at 16px and on hostile backgrounds.

2 · Fifteen sketches: SZV plus one more idea

Every sketch keeps your one-line SZV and pairs it with a noun from the list. Rough, not precious.

3 · Top three, refined and pressure-tested

Each one is shown on your navy and cream, in one colour, on a deliberately hostile background, as a lockup with your name, and at 96, 48, 32 and 16px.

01 · Resonance

Your one line, echoed. I took the inline technique I use on badges and ran your SZV line through it, so it reads as a voice that rings in the room. One idea, very ownable. At small sizes it hands off to the solid mark, which is how a logo system should work.

02 · On Air

The V opens like a mouth and two signal arcs come out of it. Two ideas in one mark: your initials and a broadcast. I cut it from three arcs to two; the third added nothing, and that's the line where simplifying stops.

11 · Signal

The same line, heavier, cut by three scan lines that split it into equal bands. It reads as transmission, it's bold enough to hold at 16px, and the scan lines match the width of the sliver you asked for, so it's one consistent system.

If I'm Allan: my call

01 · Resonance as the primary mark, with the solid SZV as its small-size partner. It's the only one of the three that couldn't belong to anyone else. It keeps your one-line idea and adds the thing you sell: a voice that fills the room. On Air explains itself more, but radio arcs are a category cliché. Signal is the strongest at tiny sizes, so it's the fallback if you want one mark that does everything without a partner.

He'd also push you here rather than follow: lead the client, take the creative risk. Resonance is that risk.
