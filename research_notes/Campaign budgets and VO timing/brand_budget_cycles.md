# Corporate marketing budget cycles and VO commissioning timing (brands + B2B/SaaS)

_Researched 2026-10-05. Tags: ✅ = primary source opened this session (named). 🟡 = secondary or search-snippet only, not opened. ❓ = not found. All fiscal-year dates come from SEC EDGAR's live company submissions API (`data.sec.gov/submissions/CIK##########.json`, field `fiscalYearEnd`), queried this session. Gartner's own newsroom returned HTTP 403, so Gartner figures come from trade press that reported them (named per line)._

## When do companies run annual marketing planning and budgeting, and when is budget approved and released?

### Takeaway
The real budgeting work is short: about 4–9 weeks from objectives to an approved budget. It runs in the 1–4 months before the fiscal year starts. For a calendar-year company that means roughly Sept–Nov planning, approval in Nov–Dec, and money released in January, with the first weeks of the new year slow. I found no strong primary source for the exact marketing calendar. The timing below is a well-supported rule of thumb, not a measured fact.

### Cited Findings
- ✅ The AFP 2026 FP&A Benchmarking Survey found an **average budgeting cycle of 8.7 weeks**. APQC top performers finish the annual budget in **≤25–28 days**, about half the time of 75th-percentile organizations. APQC defines the metric as calendar days from setting budget objectives to delivering a ready-to-use budget. — [CFO.com, "Cycle Time to Complete the Annual Budget: Metric of the Month"](https://www.cfo.com/news/cycle-time-to-complete-the-annual-budget-metric-of-the-month/655292/) (🟡 search snippet; the page was not opened) and [APQC measure page](https://www.apqc.org/resource-library/resource-collection/planning-and-management-accounting-key-benchmarks) (🟡 not opened)
- 🟡 Vendor and finance blogs, not primary research, describe this calendar-year pattern: "initial planning and strategic guidance ... late August or early September, and the bulk of the work accelerating throughout October." They put strategic marketing planning 4–6 months before FY start, detailed budgeting 3–4 months before, and formal approval 4–8 weeks before. — [CLIMB, "When Is Budget Season"](https://climbtheladder.com/when-is-budget-season-the-annual-corporate-timeline/); [Rajiv Gopinath, Marketing Budgeting Calendar](https://www.rajivgopinath.com/blogs/marketing-hub/marketing-data-and-measurement/budgeting-and-roi/budgeting-fundamentals/marketing-budgeting-calendar-a-month-by-month-view) (both low-authority, not opened)
- ✅ Agency buyers now see more **quarter-by-quarter planning cycles** in place of fixed annual budgets. — [Digiday, Marketing Briefing, Dec 3 2024](https://digiday.com/marketing/marketing-briefing-why-marketers-arent-focused-solely-on-use-it-or-lose-it-spending-in-q4-anymore/)
- ✅ The Gartner and CMO Survey fieldwork windows (Jan–Mar each year, below) show that big-company marketing budgets for the year are set by Q1.

### Inferences
- Calendar-FY company: pitches that land in **Sept–Oct** reach people while they write next year's line items ("budget a VO for the 2027 product launch"). Pitches in **Nov–mid-Dec** catch the flush (see below). Pitches in **mid-Jan to Feb** catch freshly released budgets once the slow first weeks have passed.
- Non-calendar FY company: move the same pattern relative to its own FY start (see the table in the next section).

### Gaps
- ❓ I found no primary, marketing-specific survey that measures *when* marketing budgets are approved or released (month distribution). The month-by-month timing above is practitioner convention, not measured data.
- ❓ I did not open the AFP 2026 report itself (paywalled). The 8.7-week figure is relayed via CFO.com.

## How common are non-calendar fiscal years among SaaS/tech/edtech companies? Real fiscal-year starts

Non-calendar fiscal years are the **norm among large enterprise SaaS**, not the exception. Salesforce, Workday, Zoom, Snowflake, CrowdStrike, Okta, Box, DocuSign, MongoDB, Asana, Braze, PagerDuty, Autodesk, Nvidia, Skillsoft and Dell all end their year at the end of January. ### Inferences
- **The year-end flush and new-budget windows by FY group** (derived from the table plus the flush and Q1 evidence below; the timing is inference):
  - FY ends Jan 31 (Salesforce, Workday, Zoom, Snowflake, Braze, PagerDuty, Skillsoft …): flush **mid-Dec → Jan**, new budget **February**, planning **Oct–Dec**. Their Q4 overlaps the calendar holidays, so a pitch in the first two weeks of January can land in a live flush window that calendar-year buyers don't have.
  - FY ends May 31 (Oracle, Nike): flush **Apr–May**, new budget **June**.
  - FY ends June 30 (Microsoft/LinkedIn, Atlassian, P&G, Estée Lauder): flush **May–June**, new budget **July**, planning **Mar–May**.
  - FY ends late July (Cisco/Splunk, Intuit, Palo Alto, Zscaler): flush **June–July**, new budget **August**.
  - FY ends late Sept/early Oct (Apple, Disney): flush **Aug–Sept**, new budget **October**.
  - FY ends late Nov (Adobe): flush **Oct–Nov**, new budget **December**.
  - FY ends Mar 31 (many UK companies, 8x8): flush **Feb–Mar**, new budget **April**.
- Spencer's pipeline is heavy on calendar-year edtech (Coursera, Udemy, Duolingo, 2U) and Jan-31 enterprise SaaS. So in practice **two** "new money" moments matter: January (calendar-year) and February (Jan-31 FY).

## "Use it or lose it" year-end flush in marketing/creative spend

### Takeaway
The flush is real and documented in trade press. It runs from roughly the week after Thanksgiving to Dec 31 for calendar-FY marketers. Since about 2022–2024 it has shrunk, because marketers have moved to quarterly planning and performance accountability. When money does surface late, buyers now want it to "perform" rather than just be parked. I found **no quantitative study** of flush size in marketing or creative spend specifically.

### Cited Findings
- ✅ Year-end "irrational budget dumping" runs **from the week after Thanksgiving to Dec 31**. Brian Krick (EVP global media planning, Essence): "It's real, it happens and has a real cost to business." Vendors (ad tech/martech) email buyers urging them to spend remaining budget before Dec 31. Companies usually don't allow rollover, and unspent budget can mean a lower budget next year. Essence estimated that spreading unspent Q4 budgets through the year could yield **5–10% more return**. An unnamed agency exec with 25+ years: "This has been happening as long as I've been in business." — [Digiday, Nov 29 2017](https://digiday.com/marketing/irrational-budget-dumping-end-year-means-use-lose-marketing-projects/) (dated 2017)
- ✅ By late 2024 the flush was "a lot less commonplace." When it happens, requests typically come from big marketers spending **$300–400M+ a year**. Mike Feldman (VaynerMedia): "Historically, we used to get like a million dollar last minute asks in the year to 'use it or lose it.'" Zach Ricchiuti (Kepler): "That just doesn't really happen anymore." Marketers now ask for performance-driven uses of late money (creator affiliates, Amazon DSP, retail-media tests), not net-new partners. Jennifer Kohl (VML): "It's not necessarily a net new partner or net new media channel." — [Digiday, Dec 3 2024](https://digiday.com/marketing/marketing-briefing-why-marketers-arent-focused-solely-on-use-it-or-lose-it-spending-in-q4-anymore/)
- 🟡 Production and creative vendors publish "spend your leftover Q4 marketing budget" content aimed at year-end buyers. Examples are video producer Lemonlight, OBI Creative, Phase 3 and Fosho Studios (UK). This is the vendor-exploitation pattern applied to *creative/video*, not just media. — [Lemonlight](https://www.lemonlight.com/blog/how-to-spend-your-leftover-marketing-budget/); [OBI Creative](https://obicreative.com/insights/leftover-marketing-budget-2/); [Phase 3](https://www.phase3mc.com/thinking/unused-marketing-budget-blog); [Fosho Studios](https://foshostudios.com/use-it-or-lose-it-how-to-maximise-your-marketing-budget-before-year-end/) (none opened; they show vendor behaviour, not evidence of size)
- 🟡 Publisher-side commentary says brands exhaust annual budgets in December, then pause in January to plan. It also describes a mini-slump at the start of *every* quarter and front-loaded spend at quarter-end to hit targets. — [Freestar](https://freestar.com/why-january-is-a-chill-month-for-publisher-ad-revenue-seasonal-trends-explained/) (vendor blog, not opened)

### Inferences
- For a freelance VO, the flush works best with **small, fast, already-approved-vendor purchases**, not a new relationship opened in December. Late money goes to "not net-new" partners (Kohl). So the useful move is to **get onto vendor lists in Sept–Nov** so you're an existing option when Dec money appears. Cold pitches in mid-December mostly arrive too late.
- Quarter-end "hit the target" behaviour suggests smaller flush windows in the last 2–3 weeks of each fiscal quarter, mainly for performance teams. This is weaker evidence for creative/VO.

### Gaps
- ❓ I found no study that measures the size of year-end flush in *corporate marketing/creative* spend. The best-known quantitative flush study (Liebman & Mahoney, US federal procurement) is government spending and was not opened this session.
- ❓ I found no data on whether production/VO vendors specifically see a December revenue spike.

## Q1 behaviour, mid-year reforecasts, freezes and cuts (2023–2026)

### Takeaway
Q1 opens slowly: the first weeks of January are quiet while teams finalise plans and set up campaigns, and Q1 ad revenue often runs behind forecast. Budgets were cut hard in 2023–24 (Gartner: 9.1% → 7.7% of revenue). They were flat in 2025 (7.7%) and 2026 (7.8%), and IT/business-services marketing budgets were squeezed especially hard. In 2025, tariff uncertainty triggered spring/Q2 cuts and pauses, mainly in consumer goods.

### Cited Findings
- ✅ Wistia 2026 State of Video (900+ respondents, 2025 activity and 2026 plans): **40% expect to increase video production spend in 2026, 46% to hold flat**. The share planning increases "has cooled since 2023." Promotion budgets: **48% plan increases, <10% reducing**. — [Wistia, State of Video 2026](https://wistia.com/blog/video-marketing-statistics)
- 🟡 Digiday: January ad revenue at some publishers paced **10–25% behind** projections. One exec: "The first two weeks were crickets." — [Digiday](https://digiday.com/media/some-publishers-report-that-q1-ad-revenue-is-pacing-10-25-behind-forecasts/) (search snippet; not opened; year of article not confirmed)
- 🟡 Gartner 2024 CMO Spend Survey (Feb–Mar 2024, n=395): budgets fell to **7.7% of revenue from 9.1% in 2023**. Ewan McIntyre: pre-pandemic average 11%, four years since 8.2%, an "era of less." **64%** lacked resources to execute 2024 strategy. — [Gartner newsroom, May 13 2024](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2024-05-13-gartner-cmo-survey-reveals-marketing-budgets-have-dropped-to-seven-point-seven-percent-of-overall-company-revenue-in-2024) (403 on fetch, search snippet only); [Marketing Dive](https://www.marketingdive.com/news/gartner-CMO-spending-survey-2024-generative-AI/716177/) (not opened)
- ✅ Gartner 2025 CMO Spend Survey: flat at **7.7%** (down from about 9.5% three years earlier). **IT/business services fell from 9% (2024) to 5.8%**, while consumer products rose from 6.7% to 9.7% and pharma from 7% to 9%. **39% of CMOs plan to cut agency allocations and 39% to cut labor costs.** — [Chief Marketer, May 2025](https://www.chiefmarketer.com/gartner-39-of-cmos-plan-to-reduce-labor-costs-and-cut-agency-allocations/)
- ✅ Gartner 2026 CMO Spend Survey (Jan–Mar 2026, n=401, mostly $1B+ revenue, North America/UK/Europe): **7.8% of revenue (from 7.7%)**, still "18% lower than the mean budget allocation just four years ago." The average **15.3% of marketing budget goes to AI initiatives** (21.3% among AI-ready firms). — [Sword and the Script, Jun 30 2026](https://www.swordandthescript.com/2026/06/gartner-cmo-trilemma/); 🟡 paid media 31.4%, labor 24.5%, martech 19.4% per search snippet of [Gartner newsroom May 11 2026](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-05-11-gartner-2026-cmo-spend-survey-finds-cmos-allocate-15-point-3-percent-of-marketing-budgets-to-ai-but-only-30-percent-are-ready-to-scale-ai-capabilities) (403 on fetch)
- 🟡 The Digiday Media Agency Report 2025 says **45%** of agency respondents saw client budgets decrease in 2025; of those, **53%** saw cuts under 20%. Forrester's Jay Pattisall expected cuts "as soon as Q2." Kimberly-Clark cut marketing spend 10.8% year on year in Q2. — [Digiday Media Agency Report 2025](https://digiday.com/media-buying/digiday-media-agency-report-2025-how-clients-are-experimenting-and-spending-from-ai-to-tariffs/); [Digiday tariffs briefing](https://digiday.com/marketing/marketers-focus-on-operational-challenges-from-tariffs-ahead-of-messaging/) (search snippets; not opened)
- 🟡 The CMO Survey (Duke/Deloitte/AMA), Spring 2025 (Jan 21–Feb 12 2025, n=281 US VP+): marketing spend **+3.3%** over the prior 12 months, with **+8.9% forecast** for the next 12. Budgets are **9.4% of revenue** (up from 7.7% the prior fall): B2B product 6.4%, B2B services 9%, B2C product 15.5%. — [The CMO Survey topline PDF 2025](https://cmosurvey.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/The_CMO_Survey-Topline_Report-2025.pdf) (not opened; figures via search summary). Note the conflict with Gartner's 7.7%: the two use different samples and definitions.

### Inferences
- **Cuts usually land at quarter boundaries.** The evidence points to Q2 cuts after a weak Q1 or a macro shock (2025 tariffs) and to H2 reforecasts. For a freelancer, this argues for pitching early in a new budget period (when money is committed but not yet spent) rather than mid-year after a reforecast.
- **B2B SaaS/IT marketing budgets are tighter than consumer** (5.8% vs 9.7% of revenue in 2025). With 39% of CMOs cutting agency allocations, that favours a direct, cheap, fast freelance option over agency-routed production. That fits Spencer's direct-to-in-house pitch.

### Gaps
- ❓ No primary data on the share of companies doing formal mid-year reforecasts, or on which month cuts are most often announced.
- ❓ I couldn't open the Gartner primary releases (403). Figures come via trade press.

## In-housing, content/video share, and agency share

### Takeaway
Most big advertisers now run in-house agencies: 82% of ANA members in 2023, doing about 61% of their work in-house. Yet 92% still use outside agencies. About three-quarters of companies make video in-house, and about a quarter outsource to freelancers or production agencies, with outsourcing growing faster in 2025. Agencies are about 21% of the marketing budget (Gartner 2025).

### Cited Findings
- ✅ ANA in-house agency report (Feb–Mar 2023, n=162, published May 2 2023): **82%** of members have in-house agencies (78% in 2018, 58% in 2013, 42% in 2008). **61%** of work is done in-house (58% in 2018). **92%** also use external agencies, and **65%** moved business from external agencies in-house over three years. **88%** reported increased in-house workload (67% "a lot"). Work moved in-house includes digital creative, internal comms and radio. The report has no freelancer or video breakdown. — [ANA](https://www.ana.net/content/show/id/79185) (dated 2023; this is the latest ANA in-house report found)
- ✅ Gartner 2025 allocation: paid media **30.6%**, martech **22.4%**, labor **21.9%**, **agencies 20.7%**. Digital is **61.1%** of total spend. — [Chief Marketer](https://www.chiefmarketer.com/gartner-39-of-cmos-plan-to-reduce-labor-costs-and-cut-agency-allocations/)
- ✅ Wistia 2026: most companies produce video in-house. About **25% outsource to freelancers or production agencies**, and both approaches grew year on year, with **outsourced production showing the bigger jump (+7%)**. Companies making 250+ videos a year are 41% more likely to rely on an in-house team. **Almost 40% spent under $5,000** on video production in 2025, and just over 30% spent more than $5,000. Over a third use AI in video workflows, and nearly 25% plan to adopt soon. **76%** publish at least one video a month. — [Wistia](https://wistia.com/blog/video-marketing-statistics)

### Inferences
- In-house video teams with small production budgets (under $5k) still buy **discrete outside inputs** such as VO talent. An in-house studio is the buyer, not a competitor, for a freelance VO. That supports pitching in-house content/creative leads directly rather than agencies.
- AI-in-video adoption (over a third already, ~15% of marketing budgets going to AI per Gartner 2026) is a real substitution risk for low-end VO in those in-house workflows.

### Gaps
- ❓ No source gives VO or audio talent as a share of video budgets.
- ❓ No ANA in-house report after 2023 was found.

## Who holds the budget for video/VO, and procurement friction (POs, onboarding, payment terms)

### Takeaway
I found no survey that cleanly says which department owns video/VO budgets. Procurement friction is real but small for small buys. Typical P-card ceilings of about $2.5k–5k let many single VO jobs skip a PO. Supplier setup can be quick (APQC median 3 days), but manual onboarding often takes 2–6 weeks. In the EU the legal default for B2B payment is 30 days, extendable to 60. The proposed 30-day hard cap has been blocked in the Council.

### Cited Findings
- 🟡 Corporate and university purchasing-card policies commonly set single-transaction limits of **$2,500–$5,000**. Purchases above about **$4,999** normally require a purchase order. — e.g., [Columbia P-card policy](https://universitypolicies.columbia.edu/content/purchasing-card-policy), [GWU](https://compliance.gwu.edu/procurement-card-p-card), [Dartmouth](https://policies.dartmouth.edu/policy/procurement-card-program-policy) (university policies used as a proxy; not opened. ❓ no corporate P-card benchmark found)
- 🟡 APQC Open Standards data: **median 3.0 calendar days** to set up a supplier in the procurement system (n≈3,047). Practitioner sources put manual vendor onboarding at **2–4 weeks, 4–6 weeks common**, with one manufacturer case at a 50-day average cut to 8 days. — relayed via search summary of [Raindrop](https://raindrop.com/how-to-fix-supplier-onboarding-from-weeks-of-email-to-days-of-automation/) and similar vendor blogs (not opened, vendor-sourced)
- ✅ EU: under the existing Late Payment Directive 2011/7/EU, businesses must pay within **60 days maximum unless otherwise expressly agreed** and not grossly unfair. The Commission's Sept 2023 proposal for a Late Payment Regulation (30-day cap) passed Parliament on **Apr 23 2024** but is **"Blocked" at the Council** (legislative train page last updated Sep 20 2026). — [European Parliament Legislative Train](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/legislative-train/theme-a-europe-fit-for-the-digital-age/file-late-payments-directive-revision)
- ❓ / 🟡 Department ownership: Vidyard research says video use is expanding from marketing into sales, but I found **no survey that breaks down budget ownership** (brand vs product marketing vs content studio vs L&D). — [Vidyard press releases](https://www.vidyard.com/press-releases/new-research-finds-use-video-accelerating-b2b-71-percent-reporting-video-converts-better-content-types/) (not opened)

### Inferences
- **Keep the first job under the buyer's card/PO threshold** (about $2.5k–5k). This lets a content lead buy without procurement and turns a cold contact into a vendor of record in days rather than weeks. Raise rates on job two and beyond, once onboarding is done. This is a working hypothesis from the P-card evidence, not tested.
- **Onboarding lag means a Q4 pitch has to land by about mid-November** to clear vendor setup before a December flush. A January pitch gives onboarding time to finish so work arrives in Feb–Mar.
- In Europe, expect net-30 by default and up to net-60 at large companies. Large US enterprises may impose longer terms. ❓ No US payment-term benchmark was opened this session.

### Gaps
- ❓ Who owns VO spend (brand, product marketing, creative studio or L&D) by company type: no data found. This needs interviews or job-post analysis.
- ❓ No primary benchmark on US corporate payment terms (DSO/DPO) for small creative vendors.
- ❓ No source on freelancer-specific onboarding steps (W-9/W-8BEN, contractor classification, security questionnaires). This matters for Spencer as a US person billing via a California LLC from Portugal.
